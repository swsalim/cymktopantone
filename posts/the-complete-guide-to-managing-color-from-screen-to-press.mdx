A blue that looks electric on a laptop can arrive on a business card looking dull, gray, or slightly green. The same brand color can also fail as body text on a website, disappear against a hover state, or shift when a PDF moves between applications. Color management for web and print prevents these surprises by giving each color a defined role, profile, medium, and approval status.

Color Mapper is a practical starting point for <Link href="/palettes">palette generation</Link>, <Link href="/gradients">gradients</Link>, <Link href="/contrast-checker">WCAG contrast checks</Link>, <Link href="/color-scale">Tailwind-style scales</Link>, and <Link href="/convert-color">conversions between RGB, CMYK, HEX, HSL, and HSV</Link>. The tools help you inspect a decision. Your printer’s output conditions still decide how ink behaves on paper.

**Quick answer:** keep a source color record, separate brand colors from interface colors, check semantic pairs against WCAG thresholds, identify the print output profile early, and approve the final PDF and physical proof against the printer’s specification.

## When this workflow does not apply

A shared color system is useful for most websites, PDFs, campaigns, and printed collateral, but the same process cannot answer every production question. Use specialist advice when:

- The job requires a named spot color, metallic ink, fluorescent ink, or a finishing process that changes appearance.
- The printer has supplied a house profile, paper-specific profile, or export specification that overrides your normal document settings.
- A legal, medical, or safety communication depends on exact physical color recognition rather than general visual consistency.
- The interface uses wide-gamut CSS colors that your browser, design software, testing devices, or delivery pipeline cannot preserve reliably.

In those cases, treat this guide as the planning layer. Confirm the production layer with the printer, prepress operator, accessibility reviewer, or design-system owner before release.

## Why the same color changes between a screen and a press

Screen and print begin with different physical conditions, so a shared number never guarantees a shared appearance. Displays emit light through red, green, and blue channels. Printed material reflects light from paper through layers of ink, usually with cyan, magenta, yellow, and black separations.

A **color space** defines how numbers describe color, such as sRGB or a CMYK space. A **color profile** describes how a particular device or output condition produces those colors. The ICC explains that device profiles help color-management systems convert native device colors through a device-independent reference space. Adobe also describes profiles as part of managed conversions between documents, displays, and output devices.

A **gamut** is the range of colors a space or device can reproduce. A **rendering intent** is the conversion strategy used when the source contains colors the destination cannot reproduce. **Proofing** is the act of reviewing a simulated or physical output before the full job runs.

Consider an illustrative electric blue such as HEX `#005BFF`. On a bright display it can look intensely saturated. A particular coated-paper press condition may not reproduce that blue at the same saturation. The approved print version could be a less saturated blue, but the actual CMYK values depend on the printer’s profile, paper, ink, and process. Convert the digital value with the <Link href="/convert-hex-to-cmyk">HEX to CMYK converter</Link> and compare the shift in the <Link href="/print-gamut">print gamut checker</Link>. Named swatches that already have a page are listed in the <Link href="/colors">color index</Link>.

There is no universal RGB-to-CMYK answer. A conversion intended for one press condition can produce a poor result on another. Check the output profile before you export, and ask the printer whether the job needs process CMYK, a spot color, or a supplied PDF standard.

A HEX value identifies a digital color reference; it does not promise identical ink on every paper.

For a reliable color management workflow, keep the source color, destination profile, conversion method, and proof status together. That record is more useful than a loose swatch pasted into a brand deck.

## Create one source of truth for brand colors before production

Without a shared color record, each team quietly invents its own version of the brand. A designer uses a HEX value, a developer samples a screenshot, and a printer receives an untagged CMYK number. The files may look close on one screen while drifting across applications.

Store each color with enough information for another person to make the same decision. At minimum, record:

- The brand or semantic role, such as signature blue, body text, link, border, or warning.
- The source value and representation, such as HEX, sRGB, or a supported OKLCH value.
- The intended medium, including web UI, digital artwork, PDF, coated print, or uncoated print.
- Contrast results for named foreground and background pairs.
- The approved print alternative, output profile, spot-color instruction, or printer note.
- The approval owner and status, such as draft, digitally approved, or press approved.

Separate identity colors from functional interface colors. A signature blue can remain central to the logo, hero artwork, or large display type while a darker companion handles paragraphs and links. That separation gives brand color accessibility a practical home instead of asking one color to perform every job.

The following is an illustrative record, not a production specification. The CMYK reference must be recalculated or confirmed for the chosen output condition.

| Field | Illustrative record |
| --- | --- |
| Brand name | Signature blue |
| Digital source | HEX #005BFF; sRGB RGB 0, 91, 255 |
| Supported modern representation | OKLCH value recorded separately where the browser and design system support it |
| Digital role | Logo, large display text, and selected decorative surfaces |
| Illustrative CMYK reference | Example only; confirm against the printer’s ICC profile and paper |
| Spot-color guidance | Ask the printer whether a spot color is needed for a high-saturation brand field |
| Accessibility note | Do not use the signature blue for small body text on white without a passing contrast result |

CSS Color Module Level 4 supports color functions and spaces beyond traditional sRGB, but a representation remains a description of color. It does not remove the need to define the output device for print. A CMYK value without an output condition is a reference, not a physical guarantee. The <Link href="/blog/oklch-for-designers">OKLCH for designers</Link> note covers the screen side of that distinction.

## Choose RGB, HEX, HSL, HSV, and CMYK for the right job

Choosing the right representation prevents errors before conversion begins. HEX is compact notation for sRGB-style channel values, while RGB exposes those channels directly. CSS can also express newer color spaces and functions when browser support and the delivery pipeline allow them, as described in the <a href="https://www.w3.org/TR/css-color/" target="_blank" rel="noreferrer">CSS Color Module Level 4 specification</a>.

- **HEX:** useful for storing familiar web colors in CSS, design documentation, and simple handoffs. See the <Link href="/color-models/hex">HEX guide</Link>.
- **RGB:** useful when software, APIs, or image tools need explicit red, green, and blue channel values. See the <Link href="/color-models/rgb">RGB guide</Link>.
- **HSL:** useful for describing hue, saturation, and lightness relationships during editing. See the <Link href="/color-models/hsl">HSL guide</Link>.
- **HSV:** useful in some picker and grading workflows, especially when a team already uses that model. Convert with <Link href="/convert-hsl-to-hsv">HSL to HSV</Link> rather than pasting the numbers across. See the <Link href="/color-models/hsv">HSV guide</Link>.
- **CMYK:** useful for print production when paired with the output profile, paper, and press specification. See the <Link href="/color-models/cmyk">CMYK guide</Link> and <Link href="/blog/cmyk-safe-web-palettes">CMYK-safe web palettes</Link>.

HSL and HSV make color relationships easier to discuss, but equal numeric steps do not create equal perceived changes. They also do not automatically produce accessible or print-safe colors. A scale that moves lightness by the same amount can look uneven, especially across different hues. Build a candidate ramp in the <Link href="/color-scale">tint and shade generator</Link>, then measure the pairs.

For example, a developer might store a web link as `#0057B8`, while a printer receives a profiled CMYK conversion for the brochure. Both values can represent the same semantic role, yet they belong to different output conditions. Do not copy a CMYK value from one application into another without checking document profiles and color-management settings.

Request the printer’s ICC profile when the job depends on accurate color fields, photographs, gradients, or a close brand match. The ICC profile describes device and media behavior; it is not merely a table of universal replacements.

## Handle the color gamut for print before the design is finished

Late gamut checks force avoidable compromises into the final hour. Check the color gamut for print while large fields, gradients, photography, and brand colors can still change without a costly redesign. The <Link href="/print-gamut">print gamut checker</Link> shows the site’s screen-to-CMYK shift beside the original. Use it to spot a problem, then confirm with the printer’s profile.

An out-of-gamut color sits outside the destination condition’s reproducible range. A browser may display a highly saturated blue that a CMYK press cannot reproduce at the same brightness and saturation. The conversion must map it to a printable result, and different rendering intents can make different tradeoffs.

Use the intended print profile for a soft proof where your software supports it. A soft proof can reveal clipped saturation, shifted neutrals, and weak contrast before export. It cannot predict every press result because paper whiteness, ink limits, calibration, viewing light, drying, coating, and finishing also affect appearance.

For an illustrative electric blue used across a landing page and a brochure, test four decisions:

1. Keep the original digital blue for the website if the target displays and browser support handle it.
2. Reduce saturation while preserving the brand’s visual direction for a process-print field.
3. Choose a nearby approved print color that holds its appearance on the selected paper.
4. Ask about a spot color when the brand field needs a closer match and the job supports that ink.

Prefer one approved print alternative over ad hoc adjustments by each designer. Record the profile, paper, conversion intent, and proof decision beside the swatch. That record turns the web-to-print workflow into a repeatable production step.

Check the destination profile before final artwork, because saturated colors are hardest to repair after layout approval.

## Build brand color accessibility into the palette

A palette can look coherent and still fail when people read, focus, hover, or interpret data. Brand color accessibility starts with semantic pairs, not a general impression that the swatches look distinct. The <Link href="/blog/wcag-contrast-brand-colors">WCAG contrast guide</Link> is the shorter version of this section.

WCAG 2.2 Success Criterion 1.4.3 generally requires a contrast ratio of at least **4.5:1 for normal text** and **3:1 for large text**. Large text uses the criterion’s defined size and weight conditions. The criterion includes exceptions for incidental text, inactive controls, pure decoration, and logotypes. Read the <a href="https://www.w3.org/TR/WCAG22/#contrast-minimum" target="_blank" rel="noreferrer">WCAG 2.2 Success Criterion 1.4.3</a> and the W3C’s <a href="https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/contrast-minimum.html" target="_blank" rel="noreferrer">contrast guidance</a> before setting an internal rule.

The contrast ratio compares relative luminance, which means hue alone cannot rescue a faint text color. WCAG color contrast ratios help you test defined cases, but they cannot guarantee comfortable reading for every person, screen, or lighting condition. Run the pair in the <Link href="/contrast-checker">contrast checker</Link>.

Work through a brand example. Suppose the signature blue is `#005BFF` and it fails the normal-text check against white. Keep it for the logo, a large headline after checking its size, or decorative artwork. Add a darker companion blue such as `#003A8C` as an illustrative candidate for body text and links, then verify the actual pair in your testing tool. Never approve the candidate from appearance alone.

Check each functional case separately:

- Body text and headings against their surfaces.
- Links in default, hover, visited, and focus states.
- Icons, borders, controls, and focus indicators where the applicable accessibility requirement covers them.
- Charts and status colors with labels, patterns, or symbols so color alone does not carry meaning. Preview collapsed hues in the <Link href="/color-blindness">color blindness simulator</Link>.
- Disabled or decorative content according to the relevant WCAG exception, without copying that exception to active content.

A chart that separates red revenue from green expenses by color alone excludes readers who cannot distinguish the hues. Add direct labels, line styles, patterns, or a table. Color palette accessibility belongs in the component behavior and content design, not just in the swatch library. The <Link href="/blog/color-blindness-simulation-common-design-questions-answered">color blindness simulation guide</Link> covers that review.

## Use a design token color scale that survives real interfaces

Raw color names create fragile interfaces. A component should ask for a semantic role such as text, link, surface, or focus, so the system can change a value without hunting through every button and card.

A design token color scale should support real states: default, hover, active, disabled, focus, error, success, warning, and dark mode. Equal numeric steps do not guarantee equal visual differences. CSS Color Module Level 4 documents interpolation behavior across color spaces, so choose an interpolation space deliberately and measure the resulting pairs. The <Link href="/blog/tailwind-color-scale-guide">Tailwind color scale guide</Link> shows one way to turn a single hex into steps.

Here is a compact illustrative token set. The syntax and build behavior depend on the design-system platform.

```css
:root {
  --brand-blue: #005BFF;
  --brand-blue-print: profile-confirmed-value;
  --color-surface: #FFFFFF;
  --color-text: #172033;
  --color-text-muted: #4B5568;
  --color-link: #003A8C;
  --color-focus: #7A3FF2;
  --color-border: #667085;
  --color-success: #176B3A;
  --color-warning: #7A4B00;
  --color-danger: #A3212B;
}
```

Approve the tokens as pairs. For example, verify `--color-text` on `--color-surface`, `--color-link` on the surface, focus against adjacent surfaces, and each status color with its text or icon treatment. Record the tested ratio and state in the design-system documentation.

This approach gives brand color accessibility a durable structure. The identity token remains available, while usage tokens carry the stricter requirements for reading and interaction. A generated scale can speed up exploration, but approval still belongs to measured semantic pairs.

Accessible tokens describe approved relationships between roles, not attractive shade numbers sitting alone in a palette.

## Run a web to print color workflow that catches problems early

A reliable web-to-print color workflow makes the output condition known before artwork reaches final export. Use this sequence:

1. **Define the source of truth.** Record identity colors, semantic UI roles, digital values, print alternatives, and approval status.
2. **Identify the destination.** Ask for paper stock, press process, output profile, bleed, overprint, and spot-color requirements.
3. **Build the digital system.** Assign accessible tokens to text, links, surfaces, controls, and states. Test WCAG contrast ratios for each required pair.
4. **Check gamut early.** Soft-proof large fields, gradients, photos, and saturated brand colors against the intended print profile. Use the <Link href="/print-gamut">print gamut checker</Link> as a first look.
5. **Prepare the PDF.** Follow the printer’s requested conversion, profile, bleed, and PDF settings. Adobe’s <a href="https://helpx.adobe.com/acrobat/using/color-management.html" target="_blank" rel="noreferrer">Acrobat color-management guidance</a> explains why PDF color handling depends on profiles and output intent.
6. **Review a physical proof.** Compare the proof under suitable viewing conditions before approving the full run.

Suppose a campaign includes a landing page, downloadable PDF, and business card. The semantic role stays stable: blue identifies the brand and a darker blue identifies links or small text. The website uses its approved digital value. The PDF carries the required profile and medium-specific conversion. The business card uses the printer-approved value for its paper and press.

That is consistency without pretending that every medium can share one raw number. The printer owns decisions about stock, ink limits, press calibration, finishing, overprint, and spot colors. The designer owns the source record, semantic roles, artwork review, and proof questions. The developer owns token implementation and browser-state checks.

| Stage | Owner | Approval artifact |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Source palette | Brand and design team | Color record with roles and status |
| Web interface | Design and development | Contrast results for text and states |
| Print setup | Designer and printer | Paper, process, profile, and spot-color decision |
| PDF export | Designer or prepress | Profile and output-setting record |
| Final approval | Brand owner and printer | Reviewed physical proof |

## Check accessibility, profiles, and exports before delivery

Final QA catches two different classes of failure: a digital interaction that excludes people and a print file that changes color between applications. Run them as separate checks, then store the evidence together.

Use this delivery checklist:

- Test normal text at 4.5:1 or higher where WCAG 2.2 SC 1.4.3 applies.
- Test large text at 3:1 or higher where the criterion’s large-text definition applies.
- Check default, hover, visited, active, disabled, and focus states.
- Confirm that charts, alerts, and status messages use labels, shapes, patterns, or text in addition to color.
- Verify the assigned document profile, converted images, embedded profiles, and output intent.
- Inspect overprint, transparency, bleed, and spot-color handling against the printer’s instructions.
- Review the PDF in the intended viewer and compare the physical proof under suitable light.
- Record the tested pair, profile, export settings, approver, and date.

Typical failure signs point to different fixes. A washed-out PDF may have the wrong output profile or an unintended conversion. A logo that shifts between applications may lack a profile or use different color-management settings. A link that passes in its default state can fail on hover. A red-versus-green chart needs a second distinction even when both colors pass a basic check.

WCAG color contrast ratios answer a web accessibility question. They do not prove that a printed blue will match a screen or that a physical sign remains readable under every light. Likewise, a successful proof review does not approve an interface’s focus state.

## Use color tools without treating their output as a press proof

Color Mapper is useful at the exploration and handoff stages. Designers can generate palettes and gradients, developers can check WCAG contrast, and teams can build scales or convert between RGB, CMYK, HEX, HSL, and HSV. No account is required.

Use the tools to test the intended foreground and background pair, compare a signature color with a darker text companion, generate a design token color scale, or create reference values for a print discussion. Label converted CMYK values as references until the printer confirms the output profile and paper. Named CSS colors and common neutrals are listed on the <Link href="/colors">color index</Link> so a hex is not the only way to find a swatch.

Color Mapper does not replace a press proof. The final printed result still depends on the selected profile, paper, ink, press process, viewing conditions, and finishing. Treat the output as a decision aid, then pass the approved token or print value to the development or print team with its role and production notes.

Start with one real campaign color. Test its web pairs in the contrast checker, record the passing semantic tokens, identify the printer’s output condition, create the medium-specific print reference, and approve the physical proof before release.
