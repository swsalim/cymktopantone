Struggling to get your colours right in print? You are not alone. Picking between spot colour and process colour is not just a technical choice—it is what separates bland, unpredictable results from sharp, brand-consistent work.

Let us break each down so you can plan print colours with fewer surprises.

## Why Knowing the Difference Matters

Colour makes or breaks print. A logo that is supposed to signal trust can look flat if the colours shift from run to run.

Spot colour and process colour each have strengths, costs, and trade-offs, and choosing wrong can blow up deadlines and budgets.

Brands, packaging, and marketing materials all ride on this decision—and your printer cares too.

## Spot Colour: Pre-Mixed Ink for Exact Hues

Spot colour is a **pre-mixed ink**—like grabbing a can of paint with an exact recipe. You get pure, vibrant colour with less guesswork because the ink is mixed before it hits the press. Many print shops use standardized spot-colour books so designers and prepress can agree on a single reference swatch.

Need a signature brand green or red that must match across regions? Spot colour is how teams keep that shade consistent when CMYK alone cannot hit the target.

Spot colours work best when:

* You need **strong accuracy** for logos and identity colours.

* Metallics, fluorescents, or specialty inks are in play.

* Consistency across print runs is vital.

* Your design uses only a few flat colours, not a full photograph.

Spot colours use separate plates on offset presses, which adds cost as you add colours—but the payoff is reliability when the hue matters more than photo realism.

## Process Colour: CMYK for Full-Colour Work

Process colour blends four inks—cyan, magenta, yellow, and black (<Link href="/color-models/cmyk">CMYK</Link>)—in different percentages and tiny halftone dots.

Millions of colour combinations come from those four inks, which is why magazines, brochures, and photo-heavy jobs rely on process printing.

Process colour shines when:

* **Full-colour photos, complex graphics, or gradients** dominate the layout.

* The project has many colours but a tight budget.

* You are on short runs or digital print where CMYK is the default.

**One tradeoff:** Process colour cannot always deliver every vivid brand hue. Ink, paper, and press conditions shift slightly between runs, so expect small variation unless you add a spot plate for critical brand colours.

## Which Method Fits Your Job?

Picture two jobs:

* Job 1: Business cards with a company logo in two brand colours.

* Job 2: Product catalog packed with full-colour photos and gradients.

For job 1, spot colour (or spot plus black) is often the right call—the logo reads in the exact shade every time.

For job 2, process colour is the practical choice—you get realistic images on every page even if non-critical colours shift a little between runs.

## How to Choose (and Avoid Costly Mistakes)

* **Audit your design first.** Is colour accuracy critical for a logo? Do photos need to look natural? Decide what must be exact vs. what can flex.

* **Talk with your printer.** Ask about spot vs process options, plate costs, and paper recommendations.

* **Preview with proofs.** What you see on screen is not what comes off the press. Request printed proofs on the actual stock when the job matters.

* **Consider your brand palette.** If a primary colour falls outside CMYK’s range, a spot plate is often smarter than forcing process CMYK alone.

* **Balance cost vs result.** Extra spot plates cost more; large photo runs usually stay cheaper in process CMYK.

## Conclusion

Spot colour delivers precision for flat, critical hues; process colour handles full-spectrum photography and gradients on a budget. Pick based on what the design actually needs.

Practical takeaway: lean spot for exact brand marks and high-impact flat colour; go process for photos and rich CMYK work. When in doubt, ask your printer and proof early.
